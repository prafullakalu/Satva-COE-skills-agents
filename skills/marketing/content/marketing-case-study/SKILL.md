# Case study writing

A case study is evidence for a buyer who sees themselves in the customer. Specificity and honest numbers make it believable; polish makes it forgettable.

## 1. Select the story
Pick a project where: the customer resembles an ICP segment; the starting pain is common; the outcome is measurable; and you can get permission. Prefer a hard problem solved over a smooth one. Check relationship health and contract confidentiality before asking.

## 2. Get permission up front
Ask in writing for: naming rights (company, people, logo), quote approval, metric approval, and use channels (site, deck, social, ads, press). Offer an anonymised version as fallback ("a UK multichannel retailer, 40 staff"). Never publish a name or a number the customer did not approve. Store approvals with the draft.

## 3. Collect the facts
Before the interview, gather from project records: scope, timeline, systems involved, baseline metrics, and after-state metrics with date ranges and measurement method. Metrics worth capturing: hours saved per period, error or reconciliation-difference rate, days to close, orders processed, cost avoided, revenue or margin impact, time to go-live.
Rule: every number needs a baseline, a result, a period, and who measured it. If the customer cannot supply them, use qualitative outcomes and say so; do not estimate and present as fact.

## 4. Interview the customer (30-40 min)
Questions, in order:
1. Tell me about your business and your role.
2. What was happening that made you look for help? What was it costing you?
3. What else did you consider or try? Why did you choose us?
4. What worried you before starting?
5. What was the work like? Any moment that stood out?
6. What changed? Give me a number or a before/after.
7. What surprised you? What would you tell a peer in your position?
Record with consent; pull verbatim quotes. Ask "can you say more about that?" on every vague answer.

## 5. Structure
| Block | Content | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | Outcome + customer type ("A retailer cut month-end close from 12 days to 4") | 1 line |
| Snapshot | Industry, size, systems, services, timeframe | 4-5 facts |
| Challenge | The situation and cost of inaction, in customer words | 80-150 words |
| Why chosen | The decision criteria | 50-100 words |
| Solution | What was built or changed, how, including a hard moment | 150-250 words |
| Results | 3 headline metrics + supporting detail + period | bullets + 80 words |
| Quote | Strongest line on outcome or relationship | 1-2 sentences |
| Next | What the customer plans next; CTA for similar buyers | 2 lines |

## 6. Write
- Customer is the hero; you are the guide. Lead with their world.
- Show the mechanism: which systems connected, which decisions made, what trade-offs. This is what peer buyers evaluate.
- Include one honest difficulty and how it was handled; it raises credibility.
- Avoid adjectives ("seamless", "game-changing"); use verbs and numbers.
- Pull quotes: lightly trim for clarity only, never rewrite meaning.

## 7. Produce the cuts
- Full page (600-900 words) and one-page PDF.
- 50-word sales snippet and slide.
- LinkedIn post (see `marketing-linkedin-b2b-content`), email blurb, pull-quote graphics.
- Rule of three: the same three metrics everywhere.

## 8. Approval and QA checklist
- [ ] Customer approved text, quotes, numbers, logo, in writing.
- [ ] Every metric has baseline, result, period, source.
- [ ] No client data, system identifiers, credentials or internal URLs visible in screenshots.
- [ ] Legal/compliance check for tax or financial claims.
- [ ] Review date set; update numbers when stale (12-18 months).

## Output
Brief fact sheet, interview guide (if not yet interviewed), draft case study, cuts, and the approvals checklist with unresolved items marked.

## Failure modes
- Vendor-as-hero narrative.
- Round, unverifiable numbers ("saved 100%").
- Publishing before customer sign-off.
- Hiding the baseline so the improvement cannot be judged.
- Writing the quote yourself.
