# Small business valuation

A valuation is an opinion supported by method, not a calculation that produces a single truth. Present a range with the reasoning visible. For large or litigated valuations a credentialed appraiser is required; label this work an indicative estimate for negotiation or planning. For public-company-style work see `dcf-model` and `comparable-company-analysis`.

## Step 1: Define the engagement

1. Subject: which entity and interest (100 percent, a minority share, specific assets).
2. Standard of value: fair market value (most tax and sale contexts), fair value (financial reporting, some legal contexts), investment value (specific buyer).
3. Premise: going concern or liquidation.
4. Valuation date and purpose. A different purpose can change the answer.

## Step 2: Normalise the earnings

1. Collect three to five years of statements and the trailing twelve months.
2. Adjust to owner-neutral earnings (see `quality-of-earnings-review` for the full bridge):
   - **SDE** (seller's discretionary earnings, for owner-operated businesses with up to roughly 1 to 2 million of earnings) = net income + interest + taxes + depreciation/amortisation + owner's compensation and benefits + non-recurring and personal expenses.
   - **EBITDA** (when the business has professional management): same, but deduct a market-rate salary for the replacement manager.
3. Weight recent years more heavily, or use the TTM, when the trend is clear; use a normalised average when earnings are volatile.
4. Check cash conversion, capex needs, working-capital needs and customer concentration since they move the multiple.

## Step 3: Market approach (multiples)

1. Use transaction databases and broker data for comparable private deals in the same industry and size, and public multiples only with a size and liquidity discount.
2. Typical ranges are industry- and size-specific: SDE multiples for small owner-operated businesses commonly run from about 1.5x to 4x; EBITDA multiples from about 3x to 8x or more for larger or recurring-revenue businesses. Verify against current data; never assert a range without a source.
3. Adjust the multiple for growth, margin quality, customer concentration, owner dependence, recurring revenue share, management depth, competitive position, asset intensity and the quality of records.
4. Revenue multiples only as a cross-check and only for businesses with predictable margins (for example recurring-revenue software or services).

## Step 4: Income approach

1. **Capitalisation of earnings:** value = normalised annual cash flow / cap rate, where cap rate = discount rate - long-term growth. Build the discount rate with a build-up method (risk-free rate + equity risk premium + size premium + company-specific risk premium). Be conservative on company-specific risk where owner dependence and concentration are high.
2. **DCF:** forecast 5 years of free cash flow to firm, estimate a terminal value (Gordon growth or exit multiple), discount at WACC; test sensitivity to discount rate and growth.
3. Reconcile: if the cap-rate result implies a multiple far from the market multiple, revisit assumptions.

## Step 5: Asset approach

Adjust book values to fair value (receivables net of doubtful, inventory at net realisable value, equipment at appraised value, intangibles) and subtract liabilities. Use as a floor for asset-heavy businesses, holding companies and where earnings are weak.

## Step 6: Reconcile and adjust

1. Weight the approaches by reliability for this business and show the weights.
2. Apply, if relevant and supportable: discount for lack of control (minority) and lack of marketability (private company); controlling interest may carry a premium. Do not stack discounts mechanically; explain each.
3. Enterprise value to equity value: subtract debt and debt-like items, add excess cash and non-operating assets, adjust for net working capital against a normal level.
4. Present a range (for example low/base/high) and a headline, with key sensitivities (earnings, multiple, discount rate) in a table.

## Failure modes

- Applying a quoted rule-of-thumb multiple with no comparables.
- Adding back real recurring costs as one-offs.
- Forgetting a replacement salary for EBITDA-based valuations.
- Double counting discounts.
- Ignoring owner dependence and customer concentration.
- Delivering a single number with no sensitivity.

## Output

Valuation memo: scope and standard of value, normalised earnings schedule, each approach with assumptions and results, reconciliation, adjustments to equity value, sensitivity table, limitations statement.
