# Real estate and property management accounting

Two business models share this vocabulary and need different books: an **owner/investor** (rental income, depreciation, debt) and a **manager** (fee revenue plus *other people's money* held in trust). Identify which you are serving before touching the ledger; a manager that mixes owner funds with its own cash has a licensing and legal problem, not an accounting one.

## Step 1: Set up

1. One entity per property or per owner (lenders and tax usually require it), with a class/location tag for each unit or building where entities are combined.
2. Chart of accounts: rental income by type (base rent, parking, late fees, pet, utilities recovery), CAM/recoveries, operating expense groups (repairs and maintenance, utilities, insurance, property tax, management fee, turnover), capital accounts (land, building, improvements, tenant improvements, furniture), accumulated depreciation, mortgage and loan costs, security deposit liability, prepaid rent liability.
3. Land is never depreciated; split purchase price between land and building on an appraisal or assessor ratio and keep the support.
4. Property managers: separate trust bank accounts for owner funds and tenant deposits; the management company's own operating account is distinct.

## Step 2: Revenue and the rent roll

1. The rent roll (unit, tenant, lease dates, rent, deposit, arrears) is the revenue subledger. Reconcile monthly: rent roll billed = GL rental income + prepaid rent change; receipts = deposits to bank; tenant ledger arrears = AR.
2. Recognise rent straight-line over the lease term for fixed escalations under lessor lease accounting (ASC 842 / IFRS 16); track the deferred straight-line rent receivable separately from billed AR.
3. Late fees recognised when charged and collectible; write off or reserve per policy at a defined aging bucket.
4. Prepaid rent is a liability until the period it covers.
5. Security deposits are a liability (and cash held in trust where law requires). Apply to unpaid rent or damage only on documented move-out, within the statutory timeline; report interest owed to tenants where required.

## Step 3: Capitalise or expense

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Repair that restores to working condition (patch, repaint a unit between tenants, replace a failed part) | Expense |
| Improvement that betters, adapts or restores (roof replacement, new HVAC, unit renovation, additions) | Capitalise; depreciate |
| Tenant improvements funded by the landlord | Capitalise; depreciate over the shorter of lease term or useful life |
| Leasing commissions, legal fees on a lease | Capitalise and amortise over the lease term |
| Acquisition costs (title, legal, survey) | Capitalise into the property basis (asset acquisition) |
| Loan fees | Debt issuance cost, amortised over loan term |

Apply a written de minimis threshold. Residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years and non-residential over 39 years straight-line for US tax; book (GAAP) lives may differ. Keep a fixed-asset register per property and consider a cost segregation study on larger acquisitions.

## Step 4: Management-company mechanics

1. Fee revenue: management fee (percentage of collections or flat), leasing fees, maintenance coordination markup, set-up fees. Recognise as services are performed.
2. Owner funds are not company revenue. Record collections and owner liabilities in trust; the monthly owner statement shows rents collected, expenses paid, fees, reserve held and the distribution.
3. Three-way trust reconciliation every month: bank balance = book balance = sum of owner and tenant-deposit ledgers. Unreconciled trust differences are an immediate escalation.
4. Never pay one owner's expense from another owner's funds. An owner with a negative balance is billed or funded before further payments.

## Step 5: CAM and expense recoveries

1. Estimate monthly CAM from the budget; bill estimates; at year-end reconcile actual recoverable expenses to estimates and pro-rate per tenant by lease share (square footage / total leasable area), applying caps, exclusions and gross-up clauses from each lease.
2. Book the true-up as a receivable or credit in the year incurred; invoice within the lease deadline.

## Step 6: Month-end checklist

Rent roll reconciled; deposits reconciled; trust accounts three-way reconciled; owner statements issued; utilities and property tax accrued; insurance prepaids amortised; depreciation posted; loan interest accrued and covenants (DSCR, LTV) calculated; vacancy and arrears report reviewed.

## Failure modes

- Security deposits swept to operating cash.
- Capitalising routine turnover costs, or expensing a roof.
- Depreciating land.
- Straight-line rent ignored, so revenue and AR drift from the lease.
- Commingling owner funds with management company funds.
- CAM true-up never run, losing recoveries after the lease window.

## Output

Reconciliation pack (rent roll, deposits, trust three-way), owner statements, capital-vs-repair ledger review, depreciation schedule per property, covenant calculation.
